20 Businesses You Can Start From Scratch — Plus the Arbitrage Playbook Nobody Writes Down

Everyone wants passive income. Almost nothing is passive. What actually exists is semi-active income: businesses with low startup costs, recurring revenue, and margins good enough that your hours are worth $100–$500 each instead of $25.

Here’s the honest version most “side hustle” lists skip: in the beginning, you are the business. You knock the doors, you make the calls, you clean the pools, you haul the junk. That’s not a bug — it’s how you learn the work well enough to eventually hire someone and know whether they’re doing it right. The good news is that almost everything on this list starts with sweat and a few hundred bucks, not a loan. Sales ability is the real startup capital. If you can get a stranger to say yes, you can build any business below from zero.

The progression is the same every time: do the work yourself, get to full days, raise prices, then hire your first helper. Most of these businesses go from “just you and a truck” to “you plus a crew” inside 12–18 months if the sales keep coming. And when you do have capital — or once you’ve stacked some profit — sites like BizBuySell are full of retiring owners selling customer lists at 2–3x earnings, which is how you buy years of door-knocking in a single transaction. But that’s the accelerant, not the entry fee.

This article is in two parts. Part 1 is twenty real businesses, graded like a report card. Part 2 is the arbitrage playbook — flips, spreads, and access plays that cost almost nothing to run because the edge is knowledge, not capital.

Part 1: The Twenty, Graded

Criteria: startup cost, time to first dollar, margin, recurring revenue, and whether it can eventually scale past your own two hands.

A-Tier: Start Here

1. Pool Route Service

The king of route businesses. Every account is $150–$250/month recurring, each stop takes 20–30 minutes once you know what you’re doing, and churn is near zero because nobody wants to touch their own chlorine. Starting from scratch: get certified (a weekend course), buy $500 in chemicals and test kits, and knock doors in neighborhoods with pools — or partner with realtors and home inspectors who see green pools every week. Twenty accounts is $3K–$5K/month for roughly two days of work. At 60–80 accounts you’re full-time and it’s time to hire a tech at $22–$28/hour so you can keep selling. Later, if you’ve got capital, existing routes sell for about 10–12x monthly revenue and you can buy growth instead of knocking for it.
Startup: $500–$2K | Income: $60K–$150K+ | Grade: A+

2. AI Managed Services Consulting

The 2026 version of the old MSP model — and the single best fit for a guy in his 40s with business experience. Doctors, dentists, law firms, and contractors know they need AI but have zero idea where to start. You walk in and say: “You don’t need to figure this out. I’ll evaluate the tools, implement them, train your staff, and manage everything for a monthly retainer.” Phone-answering AI, intake automation, review responses, scheduling, document drafting. You’re not building AI — you’re the trusted layer between vendor chaos and a busy professional. Your gray hair is an asset; a 50-year-old dentist trusts a peer, not a 23-year-old. Retainers of $1,500–$5,000/month per client are realistic, ten clients is a serious business, and the only startup cost is learning the tools — which you can do free, at night, in a month.
Startup: <$2K | Income: $100K–$500K+ | Grade: A+

3. Retail & Online Arbitrage (Amazon Flipping)

Buy low at Walmart clearance aisles, Home Depot markdowns, liquidation pallets, and closing sales — sell high on Amazon, eBay, and Facebook Marketplace. Free scanning apps show you the Amazon price and your profit before you buy, so there’s almost no guesswork. Start with $500 in inventory, reinvest every dollar of profit, and graduate into online arbitrage (buying from websites, no driving) and wholesale accounts. Ship inventory into Amazon FBA and they handle storage, shipping, and returns. It’s a treasure hunt with a P&L. The catch: it’s inventory-flipping, not recurring revenue, and Amazon’s fees take a bite. But as a “make an extra $1,500–$5,000/month starting this weekend with money already in your wallet” play, nothing beats it.
Startup: $500–$2K | Income: $20K–$100K+ | Grade: A

4. Commercial Cleaning (Janitorial Contracts)

Contract revenue — offices need cleaning every week regardless of the economy. Starting from scratch: you clean the first few accounts yourself, nights, while keeping your day job. Two or three $1,500–$2,500/month contracts is a real second income for maybe 15 hours a week. The sales motion is walking into small offices, medical suites, and gyms and asking who does their cleaning and whether they’re happy. Once you’re at capacity, your next contract pays for your first hired cleaner — and from there it’s a margin business that grows as fast as you can sell.
Startup: <$500 | Income: $60K–$250K+ | Grade: A

5. Bookkeeping Service

Every small business needs it, most owners hate it, and cloud software made it fully remote. Charge $300–$800/month per client, stack 15–25 clients, and you’ve got a high-margin recurring business with no truck, no equipment, and clients who almost never leave. Certification courses are cheap and fast, and your first clients are people you already know — the contractor buddy, the guy with the food trailer, the buddy’s wife with the boutique. Pairs beautifully with the AI consulting angle — automate half the work, keep the retainer.
Startup: <$1K | Income: $60K–$200K | Grade: A

6. Short-Term Rental Co-Hosting

Manage other people’s Airbnbs for 15–25% of revenue. You don’t own the property or carry the mortgage — you handle guest messaging, cleaner coordination, and pricing from your phone. Five properties averaging $4K/month in bookings at 20% is $4K/month, largely systematized. Vacation markets are full of absentee owners begging for this, and your pitch costs nothing: “Your listing’s sitting empty on weekdays — let me fix that and I only get paid when you do.”
Startup: $0 | Income: $30K–$150K | Grade: A

B-Tier: Solid Money, One Catch Each

7. Pressure Washing

$300–$600 for a residential driveway-and-patio job you can learn to do well in a week, and the real money is quarterly commercial contracts — HOAs, shopping centers, restaurant chains. Start with a $1,500–$3,000 rig, do every job yourself, and let before/after photos on Facebook sell the next ten jobs. It’s honest physical work — you’ll feel it — but effective rates of $100–$200/hour make it some of the best-paid sweat available. Add a helper when the schedule fills.
Startup: $1.5K–$4K | Income: $50K–$200K | Grade: B+

8. Christmas Light Installation

$1,500–$5,000 per house for install, takedown, and storage, and customers rebook every year. Ten weeks of intense seasonal work that plenty of operators turn into $50K–$100K+. It’s ladder-and-roofline work, so know your limits — start with single-story homes and ground-level displays, and bring on a younger climber for the two-story jobs as bookings stack up. Pressure washing companies bolt this on for a reason: same customers, opposite season.
Startup: $2K–$5K | Income: $50K–$150K (seasonal) | Grade: B+

9. Lawn Care / Landscaping Route

Same recurring route math as pools with a bigger customer pool, but lower tickets and more competition. Start with a used mower, trimmer, and blower — $1,500 total on Facebook Marketplace — and build density in one or two neighborhoods so you’re not driving between jobs. It’s real physical work in Texas heat; the reward is that 40 weekly accounts at $50 is $2K a week, and the moment you can’t keep up is the moment you can afford help.
Startup: $1.5K–$5K | Income: $50K–$120K | Grade: B+

10. Junk Removal

$300–$800 per truckload, half of it resellable or scrappable, demand booming from downsizing boomers and estate cleanouts. If you’ve got a truck or can borrow a trailer, you can start this Saturday. It’s heavy lifting — bring a buddy on the big jobs and split the ticket. Bonus: estate cleanouts feed your arbitrage side with free inventory (more in Part 2).
Startup: $0–$5K (if you have a truck) | Income: $60K–$150K | Grade: B

11. Gutter/Window/Exterior Add-On Bundle

Gutter cleaning, window washing, and soft-wash as a bundled recurring exterior-care package sold to the same customers as your pressure washing or lawn route. $150–$400 per visit, twice a year, almost no equipment. Weak standalone, strong as route density — every add-on service makes each customer worth more without costing another dollar of marketing.
Startup: <$1K | Grade: B

12. Loan Signing Agent / Mobile Notary

$75–$200 per appointment, near-zero startup, and the work is driving to a kitchen table and supervising signatures. Real side income at $1K–$4K/month that fits around a day job. Catch: volume swings with real estate and rates.
Startup: <$500 | Grade: B

13. Vending Machine Route

A well-placed machine nets $100–$400/month with an hour a week of restocking. Start with one used machine ($1,200–$2,500) and hustle the placement yourself — gyms, shops, break rooms — because placement is everything and that’s the sales job. Add machines from cash flow.
Startup: $1.5K–$3K per machine | Grade: B

14. Pet Waste Removal / Pet Care Route

$60–$100/month per yard, weekly recurring, dense routes, absurd customer loyalty. The lowest startup cost on the entire list — a scooper, buckets, and bags — and the easiest work to do yourself indefinitely or hand to an $18/hour helper when you’d rather be selling.
Startup: <$300 | Income: $30K–$80K | Grade: B

15. Local Lead Generation Sites

Rank simple websites for “roof repair [city]” searches and rent the leads to contractors for $500–$2,000/month per site. High margin and genuinely semi-passive once ranked. Catch: months to rank and you need real SEO skill in a shifting search landscape — this one pays in year two, not week two.
Startup: <$1K | Grade: B

16. Handyman Service → Handyman Company

If you’re handy, there is endless demand for the small jobs no contractor will show up for, at $75–$125/hour. Start solo with the tools you already own. The ceiling is your own two hands — so the long game is becoming the guy who takes the calls, quotes at $95–$135/hour, and dispatches a tradesman you pay $35–$45. Do the work now, build the company as you go.
Startup: $0–$2K | Grade: B

C-Tier: Real Businesses, Real Caveats

17. Laundromat

The classic “semi-passive” dream, and it mostly delivers — once running, it’s coin-collecting and maintenance. But entry is $100K–$500K whether you build or buy, machines break expensively, and lease terms can kill you. This is the one business on the list where capital genuinely is the entry fee, which is why it’s graded down here. File it under “later, with profits from everything else.”
Startup: $100K+ | Grade: C+ (A- if you have the capital)

18. ATM Route

$2K–$3K per machine, $300–$600/month per good location. Catch: you float your own cash in the machines, prime locations are locked up, and cash usage declines every year. Startable with one machine and hustle, but the trendline works against you.
Startup: $3K–$10K | Grade: C+

19. Residential Cleaning Agency

Huge recurring demand and you can start by cleaning houses yourself this week. The catch shows up at scale: it becomes a people-management business in a cleaning costume — turnover is brutal and one bad cleaner costs three clients. Doable, just know what the second year looks like.
Startup: <$500 | Grade: C+

20. Senior Home Care / Placement Referral

Demographics are a 30-year tailwind, and placement agencies earn $2K–$5K per referral from facilities. Being 45+ is an advantage — you’re talking to your own generation about their parents. Catch: state licensing, slow emotional sales cycles, and the care side (versus placement) is a serious labor and liability business.
Startup: $2K–$10K | Grade: C+

Part 2: The Arbitrage Playbook

Everything above is a business. Everything below is a spread — a gap between what something costs in one place and what it’s worth in another. These need almost no capital because the edge is knowledge and effort, not money. Arbitrage lives wherever the seller can’t be bothered — to sort, to grade, to part out, to drive, or to list on the right platform. You get paid for doing the annoying middle step nobody else will.

You don’t need all of these. You need one or two that fit your city, your network, and your garage space.

Collectibles: Cards and Beyond

Grading arbitrage (the big one). Buy raw Pokémon and sports cards, learn to spot the ones that will grade PSA 9 or 10, submit them for roughly $20 a card, and watch a $60 raw card come back as a $400 slab. The entire edge is condition-reading — centering, surface, corners, edges — which is a learnable skill, not a gift. The closest thing to a repeatable, scalable edge in the entire hobby.

Collection buyouts. Estate sales, divorces, and moms clearing attics sell entire collections for 20–40% of value because itemizing is work. You’re literally being paid for the labor of sorting. Facebook Marketplace “whole collection” listings are chronically mispriced, and the sellers usually just want it gone.

Sealed product aging. Buy sealed Pokémon and sports boxes at retail, sit on them 3–5 years. It’s worked for a decade — with the caveat that the 2020–21 bubble proved it’s not risk-free, and it ties up cash the whole time.

Adjacent plays with identical dynamics:

  • Retired LEGO sets — sealed sets appreciate reliably after retirement, there are literal tracking sites for it, and the boxes stack in a closet. Boring and predictable, which is the point.
  • Used golf clubs — buy full sets at estate sales and Goodwill for $40, part out the driver, putter, and irons individually on eBay for $300+.
  • Vintage watches, sealed video games, mid-tier vinyl — same estate-sale-to-eBay pipeline. The seller doesn’t know what they have; you do.

Access Arbitrage: The Diamond District Model

The strangest and most underrated category. You’re not flipping objects — you’re monetizing access to wholesale pricing or trusted relationships the public can’t reach. Every industry with a wholesale/retail wall and a trust problem has a referral layer quietly living in the gap.

  • The diamond district play. Know a guy in the jewelry trade? Referring engagement-ring buyers to a wholesale contact saves them 30–40% off retail — and the contact pays you or cuts you into the deal. Weird, old-school, and completely real in every major city’s jewelry mart.
  • Contractor referral fees. Roofers, foundation repair, and solar companies quietly pay $500–$2,000 per closed referral. If you know homeowners — through church, a neighborhood, a men’s league — you are a lead source whether you realize it or not. Insurance adjusters and realtors run this play constantly.
  • Wholesale mart access. Anyone with a resale certificate can buy at wholesale flower, jewelry, and furniture marts closed to the public. Wedding flowers that wholesale for $400 retail for $2,500. Furniture marts in North Carolina, jewelry marts everywhere.
  • Auto brokering. Dealer connections get fleet and auction pricing; you charge buyers a flat $500–$1,000 to source their car below sticker. People hate dealerships enough to happily pay the middleman.
  • Medical and dental tourism coordination. The same crown that costs $1,800 in a U.S. suburb runs $400 in border-town clinics. Coordinators take a cut for vetting the clinic and handling the logistics — trust is the product.

Timing Arbitrage: Buy the Calendar

  • Event hotel blocks. The day a major tour or playoff game is announced, book cancelable hotel rooms near the venue before the pricing algorithms catch up. Free-cancellation policies make this nearly risk-free — resell or release.
  • Seasonal goods flipping. Buy generators right after hurricane season, snowblowers in April, patio heaters in June. Sell six months later at 2x. Your only cost is garage space.
  • Christmas décor and artificial trees. 75% off on December 26, full price November 1. The most boring 4x return in America.

Information & Effort Arbitrage: Paid for the Paperwork

  • Property tax protest service. Huge in Texas and other high-appraisal states. File protests for homeowners, take 30–40% of first-year savings. Nearly pure margin, recurring annually, and most homeowners never bother on their own — the county’s own appraisal notice does your marketing.
  • Storm-season canvassing. After a major hail event, out-of-state roofing crews clear insane money. You don’t need to touch a shingle — canvass neighborhoods, book inspections, and take a per-deal cut from the crew. Pure sales, no capital.
  • Surplus funds recovery. States and counties sit on billions in foreclosure overages and unclaimed property. Finders locate the rightful owners and take a capped percentage. Regulated, paperwork-heavy, and legitimate.
  • Rebate and grant hunting. Utility and government rebates for HVAC, solar, and EV installs go unclaimed constantly because the paperwork is annoying. Contractors will pay someone who packages it for their customers.

Platform Arbitrage: Same Item, Different Market

  • Facebook Marketplace → eBay. Locals price by gut; eBay prices by market. The spread on niche items — instruments, camera gear, power tools — is routinely 2–3x.
  • eBay → Etsy. Same item, different audience, and the word “vintage” is worth about 40%.
  • Rural → urban. Trailers, tools, farm equipment, and patio sets sell 30–50% cheaper 90 minutes outside a metro. One truck run per weekend.
  • Craigslist free section → anywhere. People give away hot tubs, pianos, and sheds purely to avoid moving them. The hauling is the arbitrage — and if you started the junk removal hustle in Part 1, you’re already holding the truck keys.
  • Parting out anything. A broken $50 treadmill contains $250 in parts. Appliances, mowers, and exercise equipment all part out for multiples of their whole price, and eBay parts buyers are desperate and price-insensitive.
  • Book and textbook scanning. Thrift stores, a barcode-scanning buyback app, ship the winners. Old-school, still cash-flows.
  • Storage unit and liquidation pallet auctions. Higher variance — but the operators doing this well treat it as inventory sourcing for everything above, not a lottery ticket.

How to Actually Pick

Three questions beat any grading system:

  1. Does it bill monthly? Recurring beats one-time every time. Pool routes, cleaning contracts, AI retainers, bookkeeping, and property tax protests win because next year’s revenue is already sold. Arbitrage is the exception this list makes for speed-to-cash.
  2. Can you sell it? Every business here lives or dies on one skill: getting a stranger to say yes. The labor is learnable in weeks. The equipment is cheap or already in your garage. If you can knock a door, work a referral, or walk into an office and ask for the business, you have the only startup capital that matters.
  3. What’s the ladder out of the labor? Doing the work yourself at the start is the plan, not the problem — it’s how you learn the job, keep 100% of the margin, and fund growth without debt. But pick a business where year two can look different from year one: where the next contract pays for your first helper, and where — once you’ve stacked profit — buying a retiring owner’s customer list on BizBuySell turns two years of door-knocking into one wire transfer.

The smartest structure is a stack: an arbitrage play for fast cash this weekend, a route or retainer business you build with your own hands for durable monthly income, and one access play — a referral relationship, a wholesale connection, a protest service — running quietly in the background. None of it is passive. All of it beats trading hours for wages.

Start where you are, with what’s in the garage. Sell hard, do the work, and hire the moment the calendar’s full.
